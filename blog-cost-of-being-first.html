The Cost
of Being First

Why innovation attracts criticism before it earns credit — and what happens when you build a real estate brand that no one has seen before.

There is a tax that every pioneer pays. It doesn't show up on any balance sheet. You won't find it in a brokerage handbook. But every person who has ever done something genuinely new in a conservative industry knows exactly what I'm talking about.

The tax is paid in skepticism. In sideways glances at industry events. In the quiet raised eyebrow when you explain what you're working on. Sometimes — and this is the expensive kind — it's paid in outright criticism from people who haven't yet figured out that the thing they're dismissing is the thing they'll eventually be trying to copy.

I've paid that tax. More than once.

◆

When I Took to the Sky

The first time I flew a drone over a Connecticut property for a real estate shoot, there was no Part 107 certification. There was no industry playbook. There was no "best practices" guide published by NAR. There was just a piece of technology, a property, and a gut instinct that what buyers needed wasn't another flat walkthrough — they needed to feel the land.

I'd been doing this long enough to know that real estate photography had a ceiling problem. Every listing looked the same because every agent was shooting from the same five feet off the ground. Kitchens. Living rooms. Backyards shot from the deck. The mental model was: document the rooms. Show the buyer what's there.

But that was never what I thought real estate marketing was for.

"The goal was never to document a property. The goal was to make someone fall in love with it before they ever walked through the door."

— Mark Pires

Aerial footage did something that nothing else could: it gave buyers context. It showed them the relationship between the house and its land. The distance to water. The canopy of trees in fall. The way morning light moved across a property from the east. These were things that changed purchase decisions — and I knew it before I could prove it.

So I proved it anyway.

◆

What Happens When You're Early

The Stamford Advocate ran a feature on what I was doing. To most readers of that article, it probably looked like a feel-good story about technology. To me, it was validation of something I'd already paid for with two years of being the strange one in the room at broker meetings.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being early: you are also wrong, in ways you can't see yet. The equipment wasn't as good as it is now. The regulations were murky. The editing software was primitive. The buyers weren't entirely sure what they were watching or why it mattered.

But the thing that separates pioneers from people who just try new things is that pioneers don't need the conditions to be perfect. They need the direction to be right.

The direction was right. Today, drone footage is table stakes. Every brokerage offers it. Every agent brags about it. Entire companies have been built around the service I was figuring out in a field in Fairfield County before most of these people had heard the word "drone."

I don't say that to boast. I say it because there's a lesson in it that I think matters for anyone who is building something different — whether it's a real estate brand, a business, a content platform, or a career.

◆

Why Innovation Attracts Criticism

The most honest answer I've found: it threatens the story people are already telling about themselves.

If you're an agent who has built your entire identity around your network, your handshake deals, your Rolodex — and someone comes along with a drone and a camera crew and starts building media properties around properties and communities — that's not just a competitor. That's a challenge to the model you've invested your professional life in.

So criticism isn't really about the innovation. It's about what the innovation implies. It implies that what came before was insufficient. That there was a better way and someone found it. That the game has changed and the people who aren't changing with it are going to fall behind.

That's an uncomfortable truth. And uncomfortable truths generate defensiveness, which comes out looking like criticism.

I've learned not to take it personally.

Discover CT Was Never Just a Real Estate Strategy

When I launched Discover CT, people in the industry thought it was a marketing gimmick. A fancy way to generate leads dressed up as a YouTube channel. A Trojan horse.

Some of them weren't entirely wrong — yes, it generates leads. Yes, it builds my brand. But that framing misses what I actually built, which is something much harder to replicate than a lead funnel.

Discover CT is a point of view. It's a belief that Fairfield County deserves to be documented — really documented — the way a place you love deserves documentation. The architecture. The history. The characters. The restaurants that have been in the same family for three generations. The neighborhoods that nobody outside of Connecticut knows about.

That takes work that has nothing to do with whether a house sells this month. It requires a different relationship with the communities you serve. And it creates something that no competitor can buy their way into, because you can't manufacture genuine curiosity about a place.

"You can't manufacture genuine curiosity about a place. Either you have it or you don't."

— Mark Pires, Discover CT

House Detective: Every House Has a Story

The same principle that drove me into the sky with a drone eventually drove me to start investigating the stories behind the properties I was selling.

Every house in Fairfield County has a history. Most buyers never learn it. Most agents don't know it — or don't think it matters. I think it's one of the most important things you can give a buyer: not just the physical reality of a property, but its narrative arc.

Who built it? Why? What was this land before the house existed? Who has walked these floors, and what did they make of this place? What changes did they make, and what do those changes tell you about the priorities of the people who came before?

This is what House Detective does. And yes — it makes buyers more confident. Sellers command better prices when the story of a home is told with the care it deserves. But that's a side effect of something I find genuinely interesting, which is that property is biography. It tells you where people lived and how they lived and what they valued.

The Future of Real Estate Marketing

I'm going to make a prediction that I think will age well: the agents who thrive in the next decade are not going to be the ones with the biggest teams or the most listings or the most expensive cars in the parking lot.

They're going to be the ones who figured out that real estate is a media business now. That attention is the scarcest resource in any market — not inventory, not listings, not leads. Attention. And attention flows to the people who give it something worth holding.

The traditional brokerage model — cold calls, mass mailers, sponsored bus benches — is not dying because it was ever a good idea. It's dying because the people it's supposed to reach have learned to ignore it entirely. They scroll past it. They've been trained by the internet to route around interruptive, low-value content the same way the immune system routes around things that aren't a threat.

What cuts through? Genuine expertise delivered with genuine craft. Content that earns its place in someone's feed rather than buying it. A brand that people actually seek out rather than tolerate.

That's not the future. That's what I've been building for years.

◆

What the Cost Was Worth

I've paid the pioneer tax. The skepticism, the raised eyebrows, the "that's a nice hobby" energy from people who are now trying to figure out how to do what I've been doing for a decade.

Was it worth it?

The honest answer is that I never thought of it as a cost. The things that read as risks from the outside — building a media company inside a real estate brand, going deep on storytelling when you could be spending that energy on volume — those things didn't feel like gambles to me. They felt like the only honest way to work.

If you love the place you serve, you document it. If you believe that homes have stories, you tell them. If you see technology that could give buyers something they've never had before, you pick it up and learn how it works and build something with it.

And if people look at you sideways for a while — you let them. They come around eventually.

They always do.
